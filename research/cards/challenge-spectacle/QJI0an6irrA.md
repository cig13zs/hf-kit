## 1. Idea and promise
The title promises a crowd of celebrities and a big prize. The first line names three of them
and the rule: trapped in a red circle, last to leave wins $1,000,000 for charity (0:00-0:22,
seen). The first challenge starts at 1:29 (seen). At 1:16-1:24 the host says the challenges
are inspired by his Prime Video series, Beast Games, whose second season is out (seen): the
video doubles as a promotion. The curiosity gap is who lasts, and it resolves in a briefcase
steal-or-keep final at 34:56-40:18 (seen).

## 8. Retention mechanics: why
- **A roll call of faces.** Six stars named in seven seconds. The job is delivering proof that
  the title is true.
- **The remaining-count badge.** A number that only goes down tells the viewer how far through
  the video they are. The job is setting the rhythm, like a progress bar.
- **Games as mini-episodes.** Each game has its own rule, its own stake and its own
  eliminations. The job is resetting attention every 2-8 minutes.
- **Charity stakes.** The money goes to a cause, which makes the contestants' effort
  sympathetic. The job is building identity (inferred).
- **The steal-or-keep final.** A choice rather than a physical feat ends the video. The job is
  building tension at the end.

## 9. Outlier against control
This is the control for the challenge niche. Its edit numbers almost match the $1 vs $100M car
outlier (30.9 against 29.3 cuts/min; median 1.53s against 1.63s; −6.6 against −5.6 LUFS), and it
talks faster than both outliers (193 against 174 and 183 wpm) (seen in data). Hypothesis: the
difference is the premise. Thirty celebrities with no single protagonist is harder to care
about than two exes or a price ladder, and the whole video carries a promotion for another
show. See `niches/challenge-spectacle.md`.
